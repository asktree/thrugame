Introducing Great Work!

Prelude: Making a blockchain game with artistic merit

I wanted to challenge myself to make a game that I felt had more artistic merit if built on-chain than if not.

I've thought about blockchain games ever since at least 20171, which is when CryptoKitties was released. CryptoKitties set a trend that most games in the space have since followed, which is that the primary value added by the blockchain is: proof of authenticity, low-friction monetization, and intra-user commerce.

In 2017, the idea of spending real money to generate a virtual gizmo that you inalienably owned and could freely trade with other people was novel and interesting to many, so much in fact that I personally consider it artistically meritful. However, nearly a decade later, I think this idea by itself does not hold as much interest (though it can still be neat!).

Insofar that it does hold interest, it's often premised upon hypergrowth. The ownership layer matters because it's a speculative asset -- just think of how much your Star Atlas ship will be worth once everyone and their mother is playing. This hypergrowth is premised upon a social contract where you buy my speculative asset and I keep growing the game. I don't want to make that promise, I just want to make a fun little game, so this is not so compelling to me.

In the Ethereum era, building on-chain carried major costs and constraints. As such, blockchains were/are typically used for an ownership/commerce layer and not much actual game. But this doesn't need to be the case any longer! Gas is in many cases negligible, block times are fast, and LLMs can make efficient low-level implementations of anything you can spec.

For any turn-based games, blockchains can replace backends entirely. I find this elegant and satisfying.

What is interesting for a small game? I think longevity is interesting

With a blockchain we can have online games built for longevity; the author can die (figuratively or literally) and the game takes on a life of its own. I find that interesting.

Often, the games with the smallest communities are the most valuable. There are benefits to the ubiquity of a game like Fortnite or League of Legends, but at the same time, the game cannot form real community. Unless you're among the most elite players in the world, once you play with a random person once, you'll probably never see them again.

By contrast, in a small community, you can realistically know almost everyone else who plays the game seriously. You can gain serendipitous bonds with other players after playing together over time. You can join a chat or forum for the game and it will be a tightly knit community of people with whom you actually have something unique in common.

I grew up playing a small sandbox game online and formed lifelong bonds. A personally significant friendgroup I've joined -- which introduced me to Solana and set me and other members on our current career paths -- spawned out of a forum for a small competitive cardgame over a decade ago.

And yet, despite having such rich and long-lasting communities, these small games themselves obviously have the highest mortality rates. Games often shut down forever once maintaining their live services is no longer profitable. Thats kind of sad, right?

So I think there's a kernel of something interesting there. Players can trust that if they invest time and energy into the game, the game's infrastructure won't later go away. It's marginal -- games can die even if they are technically still playable -- but it is still kind of interesting to me.

Great Work!

I was very taken with this video about Opus Magnum tournaments. It seems like I am not the only one, as the game has seen a resurgence in popularity as a result.

Honestly, at this point you need to watch the first 7 minutes of the video to have context for the rest of this post. Video is a higher bandwidth medium than text and there is nothing I can write that would convey the same information as quickly and beautifully as this video.

All done? Okay: I think something like an Opus Magnum tournament is a great fit for a blockchain game!

  1. Optimization puzzles have a sort of timeless quality that compliments the eternality of an immutable on-chain game. It can never be known if we've really found the optimal solution, discoveries continue to be made, etcetera. Elegant!
  2. For competitive play, you just need a trusted verification layer and a leaderboard. A smart contract can do both very naturally. Convenient!
  3. You could have cash prizes that people could trust will get disbursed even if the contest runs for a long time, which would be really hard to do without a blockchain. Exciting!

I wanted to make something that Opus Magnum players would enjoy. My main approach was to basically replicate the game except tweak a few parameters so that, for the same puzzles, optimal solutions in Great Work! are different from the normal game yet familiar (I did this by changing gold costs a bit, and tweaking the number of products you have to create - thus increasing the weight of how much speed matters relative to the other factors).

Lastly, I heard on an Opus Magnum stream that in an early alpha version of the game, arms could be attached to other arms. I thought that sounded pretty interesting so I put it in the game, and removed the two other ways of achieving linear motion (tracks and pistons) in an effort to make it be more relevant. I am not good enough at the game to know how much or how little this change will matter but I hope that people enjoy it.

I named the game Great Work, the English translation of "Magnum Opus", for which Opus Magnum is named. Then I added an exclamation mark so that it reads as a congratulations, because it made me laugh. Great work!
